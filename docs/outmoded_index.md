The rapid advancement of AI coding tools (like Cursor, GitHub Copilot, and autonomous developer agents) and AI business operations is fundamentally changing the economics of building and running a company. Here is a breakdown of how this disruption will unfold over the next 24 months, highlighting specific types of companies and the shift from legacy models to new opportunities.

---

### ⏳ The Disruption Timeline

The core rule of AI disruption: **If your business's primary "moat" is the cost of basic software engineering, the manual aggregation of data, or entry-level digital communication, you are on the immediate chopping block.**

#### 3 Months: Immediate Competition & Vulnerability
In the next 90 days, the most susceptible companies are those providing "thin wrapper" AI tools, standardized digital services, and basic workflow software that a competent founder can now replicate over a weekend using AI coding assistants.

* **Susceptible Company Types:**
    * **Outbound Lead Generation Agencies:** Companies that manually scrape LinkedIn, write cold emails, and manage sequences. AI agents can do this hyper-personalized at scale for a fraction of the cost.
    * **Generic SEO Content & Copywriting Firms:** Agencies relying on junior writers for volume-based blog posts or product descriptions.
    * **"Micro-SaaS" Template Builders:** e.g., *Menu creation software for local restaurants* or *Basic invoice generators for freelancers*. AI coding makes these trivial to spin up as free tools to acquire users for other products.
    * **Tier-1 Customer Support Outsourcers (BPOs):** Companies providing baseline human chat support answering FAQs and tracking orders.

#### 6 Months: Short-Term Disruption
At the six-month mark, AI coding agents will confidently build and maintain multi-feature CRUD (Create, Read, Update, Delete) applications. The barrier to entry for niche SaaS drops to near zero.

* **Susceptible Company Types:**
    * **Boutique Web & App Development Agencies:** Shops charging $15k–$50k to build standard internal tools, e-commerce fronts, or basic mobile apps.
    * **Hyper-Niche Scheduling & Booking SaaS:** e.g., *Appointment scheduling software specifically for dog groomers or massage therapists*. When building software is cheap, generic AI agents will handle scheduling natively, bypassing the need for standalone apps.
    * **Resume Screening & Initial Candidate Outreach Platforms:** Standalone HR tools that parse resumes and match keywords. AI handles contextual matching far better natively without needing a dedicated, expensive platform.

#### 12 Months: Medium-Term Disruption
At this stage, we transition from AI generating *code* to AI executing *complex workflows*. Multi-agent systems will start replacing software that currently requires heavy human operation.

* **Susceptible Company Types:**
    * **Basic Bookkeeping & Expense Categorization Firms:** Services and software focused entirely on reconciling basic accounts and tagging expenses.
    * **Standardized Legal Contract Review Software:** Platforms that charge a premium to highlight risky clauses in NDAs or standard vendor agreements. 
    * **Data Broker & Market Research Aggregators:** Companies that charge high fees for static industry reports or lists of contacts. AI will dynamically synthesize this information on demand.
    * **Junior-Level IT Helpdesk Providers:** Companies managing password resets, software provisioning, and basic network troubleshooting.

#### 24 Months: Structural Disruption
In two years, AI will deeply penetrate complex, highly regulated, and deeply entrenched industries. The disruption shifts from "cheaper software" to **"Service-as-a-Software"**—where you don't buy a dashboard to do work; you hire an AI agent to do the work for you.

* **Susceptible Company Types:**
    * **Legacy Apartment Management Software Companies:** (As you mentioned). Clunky, feature-bloated incumbents (like Yardi or RealPage) that require weeks of training to use. They will be challenged by AI-native platforms where property managers simply type, "Run background checks on the new applicants for Unit 4B, draft their lease, and send the maintenance team to fix the sink in Unit 2A."
    * **Mid-Market ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) Integrators:** Consulting firms that charge millions to customize Salesforce, SAP, or NetSuite for mid-sized manufacturers. AI will allow companies to auto-generate custom ERPs that fit their exact workflows dynamically.
    * **Medical Billing & Coding Outsourcing Firms:** Massive BPOs dedicated to translating doctors' notes into insurance billing codes.

## Publicly Traded Companies

I’m specifically looking for public companies that are at risk of being outmoded.

It makes complete sense to look at the public markets. Wall Street is absolutely ruthless when it comes to pricing in existential threats, and we are currently seeing a massive repricing of companies whose core business models are threatened by AI agents and autonomous coding. 

In early 2026, the market has even coined terms like the "SaaSpocalypse" and "vibe-coding" (where non-engineers build software using AI) to describe the panic hitting certain sectors. 

Here is a breakdown of the specific types of public companies and ticker symbols facing the most immediate existential risk from AI disruption over the next few quarters.

### 1. Business Process Outsourcing (BPOs) & IT Services
These companies built massive valuations by leveraging labor arbitrage—hiring thousands of workers in emerging markets to handle customer service, basic IT, and back-office data entry. AI agents don't just do this cheaper; they do it instantly and don't require sleep.
* **Specific Examples:** **Teleperformance (TEP.PA)**, **TaskUs (TASK)**, and major Indian IT service providers like **Infosys (INFY)**, **Wipro (WIT)**, and **eClerx**.
* **The Disruption:** Early 2026 saw massive sell-offs in this sector following the release of advanced agentic AI models from Anthropic and OpenAI. The realization is that enterprise clients no longer need to outsource tier-1 support or data reconciliation to humans; they can simply deploy a multi-agent AI system. 

### 2. Digital Freelance Marketplaces
The "gig economy" for digital services is being hollowed out from the bottom up. The bulk of the transaction volume on these platforms historically came from small businesses needing quick, inexpensive digital work.
* **Specific Examples:** **Fiverr (FVRR)** and **Upwork (UPWK)**.
* **The Disruption:** The services that used to cost $50 and take three days on Fiverr—like basic SEO copywriting, simple Python scripts, translation, voiceovers, and logo design—can now be done in 30 seconds for fractions of a penny using AI. The "middleman" platform for cheap digital labor is highly susceptible to being outmoded.

### 3. EdTech & Static Knowledge Gatekeepers
If your business model relies on charging students for access to a database of homework answers or standardized test prep, you are competing directly against free, hyper-personalized AI tutors. 
* **Specific Examples:** **Chegg (CHGG)** and **Pearson (PSO)**.
* **The Disruption:** This was one of the first sectors to take a direct hit, with companies like Chegg losing vast amounts of market cap almost immediately after generative AI hit the mainstream. AI doesn't just give the answer; it can act as a Socratic tutor, explaining the *why* behind a complex physics equation or a line of code, rendering static study materials obsolete.

### 4. Legal Tech & Standardized Document Services
Companies that charge a premium to generate boilerplate legal documents or synthesize legal research are fundamentally selling "text processing." 
* **Specific Examples:** **LegalZoom (LZ)**, **Thomson Reuters (TRI)**, and **RELX (RELX)**.
* **The Disruption:** AI models are exceptional at reading millions of pages of case law, summarizing them, and drafting standard operating agreements, NDAs, and LLC formations. While complex, high-stakes corporate law is safe for now, the consumer-facing and small-business legal tech market is highly vulnerable. In early 2026, stocks in this sector experienced sudden, steep drops as AI's ability to handle complex information tasks became undeniable.

### 5. "Thin" SaaS and Legacy Workflow Platforms
This is the broadest category, represented by the recent downward pressure on the **iShares Expanded Tech-Software Sector ETF (IGV)**. 
* **Specific Examples:** Older HR platforms, mid-tier CRMs, and niche workflow tools (like legacy property management or basic accounting software). 
* **The Disruption:** The market is waking up to the fact that many B2B software platforms are just pretty interfaces built on top of a database. With AI coding assistants, a single competent developer (or even a founder using AI tools) can replicate a SaaS product that used to take a team of 50 engineers to build. If a software company doesn't have a deep, proprietary data advantage or high switching costs, they are at risk of being replaced by cheap, custom-generated internal tools.

## Metrics

What metrics can be pulled from published financial reports, and what algorithms can be used to determine that a company is likely to be outmoded? Develop a system to read a financial report and give the company an Outmode score.

This is exactly how quantitative analysts and hedge funds are currently re-evaluating their portfolios. Traditional valuation models rely heavily on historical cash flows, but in a period of rapid technological disruption, past performance masks existential risk. 

To determine if a company is becoming outmoded, you must look for the "financial fingerprints" of inefficiency, administrative bloat, and loss of pricing power. 

Here is how you can build a systematic framework—extracting specific metrics from 10-K and 10-Q filings, applying algorithmic analysis, and calculating an **Outmode Score**.

---

### Phase 1: The Core Metrics (The Inputs)
You need to pull metrics that specifically highlight the friction of human labor and legacy technology compared to AI-native efficiency.

1. **Revenue per Employee (The Automation Index):** * *Where to find it:* Total Revenue (Income Statement) divided by Total Headcount (usually in the 10-K Business Overview).
   * *Why it matters:* AI-native companies have massive revenue-per-employee ratios. If an incumbent's ratio is stagnant while the industry average rises, they are throwing human labor at problems AI should be solving.
2. **SG&A to Revenue Ratio (The Bloat Metric):**
   * *Where to find it:* Selling, General & Administrative Expenses / Total Revenue (Income Statement).
   * *Why it matters:* AI drastically reduces the cost of sales, marketing, and back-office admin. A rising or stubbornly high SG&A ratio means the company is failing to automate internal workflows.
3. **Gross Margin Compression (The Pricing Power Proxy):**
   * *Where to find it:* (Revenue - Cost of Goods Sold) / Revenue. Track the year-over-year (YoY) change.
   * *Why it matters:* This is the canary in the coal mine. AI competitors can offer the same service at near-zero marginal cost. If an incumbent is forced to drop prices to compete but can't lower their human-centric COGS, their gross margin will shrink.
4. **Capitalized Software Impairments (The Legacy Tech Trap):**
   * *Where to find it:* "Impairment of Intangible Assets" (Cash Flow Statement or notes to the Balance Sheet).
   * *Why it matters:* When a company realizes the expensive internal software they spent 5 years building is now worthless compared to a cheap AI agent, they have to write down its value. Sudden write-downs are a major red flag.
5. **R&D Yield:**
   * *Where to find it:* Current Year Revenue Growth / Previous Year R&D Expense.
   * *Why it matters:* It measures how effectively their innovation spend translates to growth. High R&D spend with zero revenue growth implies they are patching legacy spaghetti code rather than building disruptive new features.

---

### Phase 2: The Algorithms (The Engine)
Raw numbers aren't enough; you need algorithms to contextualize the data against the broader market.

1. **Time-Series Anomaly Detection (e.g., Isolation Forests):** * Use this to track the company's Gross Margin and Revenue per Employee over a rolling 8-quarter period. The algorithm flags non-linear, negative deviations from the company's historical baseline, signaling sudden disruption rather than normal cyclicality.
2. **Peer-Benchmarking Z-Score Transformation:** * An algorithm to standardize a company’s metrics against its specific sub-sector (e.g., comparing an apartment management software company only to other property tech firms). It converts raw metrics into standard deviations from the mean to see exactly how far behind they are falling.
3. **NLP Sentiment & Keyword Extraction (Large Language Models):**
   * Apply an LLM to the "Management's Discussion and Analysis" (MD&A) and "Risk Factors" sections of the 10-K. 
   * *The Algorithm:* Train it to classify the context of the word "AI". Is it used *offensively* ("We released a new AI agent that increased conversions by 20%") or *defensively* ("Our business may be adversely affected by new artificial intelligence technologies")?

---

### Phase 3: The System — Calculating the "Outmode Score"
The Outmode Score is a probability index from **0 to 100**, where 100 means imminent obsolescence and 0 means highly resilient/disruptive. 

You can calculate this by taking the normalized peer-benchmarked metrics (where poor performance yields a higher value) and applying a weighted formula.

**The Outmode Score Formula:**

$$Outmode\ Score = \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} (w_i \cdot \hat{z}_i) \right) + (\alpha \cdot NLP_{risk})$$

*Where:*
* $\hat{z}_i$ = The inverted Z-score of the company’s financial metric compared to the industry mean (e.g., if Gross Margin is vastly underperforming the sector, $\hat{z}$ is strongly positive).
* $w_i$ = The assigned weight for each financial metric (e.g., Margin Compression gets a high weight of 0.35, SG&A bloat gets 0.25).
* $NLP_{risk}$ = A score from 0 to 20 generated by the NLP analysis of the MD&A section based on defensive posturing.
* $\alpha$ = A scaling multiplier for the NLP score.

**Interpreting the Score:**
* **0–30 (Safe/Innovator):** Expanding margins, climbing revenue per employee, aggressive AI R&D. (e.g., The disruptor).
* **31–60 (Vulnerable):** Stagnant growth, high SG&A, starting to lose market share to cheaper tools. Requires a hard pivot.
* **61–85 (Outmoding in Progress):** Margins are compressing, write-downs are occurring, management is citing tech disruption as a primary risk factor.
* **86–100 (Terminal):** Fundamentally broken unit economics. The cost to service a client is higher than the price AI competitors charge for the whole product.

### 🔍 The "AI Victim" Disruption Analysis

The core of CoStar's Outmode Score is driven by three specific vulnerabilities regarding how AI breaks their historical business model. 

#### 1. The Evaporation of the "Brute-Force" Data Moat
CoStar’s historical monopoly in commercial real estate (CRE) was built on expensive human labor. They amassed their database by employing thousands of human researchers and fleets of field photographers to make phone calls to brokers, verify lease terms, track tenant movements, and manually input data. 
* **The AI Threat:** The market is waking up to the "AI victim" narrative because LLMs and autonomous agents fundamentally commoditize this moat. AI agents do not need a 3,000-person call center. They can dynamically scrape unstructured broker PDFs, synthesize local municipal public records, ingest satellite data, and cross-reference alternative data feeds to build accurate comps instantly. If an AI-native startup can generate an equivalent CRE database dynamically for a fraction of the cost, CoStar's immense pricing power will break.

#### 2. Severe Margin Compression & SG&A Bloat
CoStar is suffering from the exact financial fingerprints of an outmoding company: rising revenues masking shrinking profitability. To brute-force their way into the residential market against Zillow, they have poured hundreds of millions of dollars into traditional marketing (Super Bowl ads, mass media). Furthermore, they recently expanded their human sales team by 20%. They are scaling expensive human headcount and legacy marketing exactly when AI-native disruptors are using autonomous outbound agents and automated marketing to drop customer acquisition costs (CAC) to near zero. 

#### 3. The Interface Vulnerability (The Portal Trap)
A massive chunk of CoStar's revenue comes from charging brokers steep fees for premium listings on their static portals (LoopNet, Apartments.com). This relies on a legacy web paradigm: a user goes to a URL, clicks drop-down filters, and scrolls through pages of listings.
* **The AI Threat:** Search is shifting to conversational and agentic workflows. If an expanding logistics company uses an AI enterprise agent and says, "Find me 10,000 square feet of flex warehouse space in Austin, verify the zoning for hazardous materials, and auto-draft initial letters of intent to the listing brokers," the AI does the work natively. It bypasses the LoopNet interface and its ad inventory entirely, stripping CoStar of its gatekeeper status.
